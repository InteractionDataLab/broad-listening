About this project

A pipeline for finding themes in open-ended texts, and two worked examples of what it produces.

What this site shows

This site shows a method for reading large collections of short texts. The method groups the texts into themes, and an interactive dashboard lets a reader move from the whole collection to a single text and back. We applied it to two collections: open answers from a survey of iGEM participants, and public reports of Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF). The method is described in a concept note, “A broad-listening pipeline for the thematic analysis of qualitative text data: the cases of open-ended survey answers and public reports” (Nuruzzaman, Tupikina and Santolini, 2026).

Who made it

The work was carried out by Md Nuruzzaman during two internships. It began in the first year of the master’s programme AIRE at Université Paris Cité, within the ANR project Open Futures, and continued at the Interaction Data Lab. The data

Survey answers. The iGEM 2025 Jamboree took place in Paris. Afterwards, 332 participants from 125 teams answered five open questions, which gave 1,505 answers. On this site the answers are shown with the names of people, teams and places replaced by a tag in square brackets, such as [name] or [city]. No team name, participant identifier or country is stored with an answer, and groups appear only as counts and shares of people. Showing the answers in this form was agreed with Marc Santolini in November 2026.

Public reports. On 16 September 2026 we collected 1,481 reports published by MSF on ReliefWeb, for eight countries and the years 2023 to 2025. Of these, 1,362 had usable text. Many stories exist in several languages or versions, so we kept each story once: 893 stories, cut into 3,912 passages. Every passage links to its source. The texts belong to MSF. This site is not made by MSF and is not linked to it.

How the themes were made

Each text is turned into a list of numbers by a multilingual embedding model, so that texts with a similar meaning get similar numbers. A grouping method then finds crowded areas and merges close areas into themes. A language model proposes a name for each theme, and a name is kept only if it passes separate checks. Groups of people or places are compared with permutation tests, corrected for the number of tests. The report and the data folder give the details.

What the results can and cannot tell

The themes and their names were produced by machine, and they have not yet been compared with a sample coded by a person. A share shows how often a topic appears in the texts. It does not show how common the topic is in the world. A difference between groups is an association and not a cause. About half of the MSF passages were first left unplaced by the grouping method and were joined to the nearest sub-theme afterwards. Each page lists its own limits.
