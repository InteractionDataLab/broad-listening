Case study 1 · iGEM 2025 survey

What iGEM participants said about their year

Each dot is one answer from the iGEM 2025 survey. Answers that say similar things sit close together, and each colour is a theme.

1,505 answers · 332 people · 125 teams · 5 questions · 7 themes

After the 2025 Jamboree in Paris, 332 participants from 125 teams answered five open questions. We placed their 1,505 answers on a map by meaning, grouped them into themes, and compared groups of teams. Team names and people’s names have been taken out. Themes and tone

What did people talk about, and how did it feel?

Four themes each reach 6 to 7 people in 10. Only two lean negative: “Financial Limitations and Funding Issues” (60% of its answers) and “Time Constraints and School Pressure” (50%).

Bar = share of the 328 people with a themed answer who raised the theme; colour = tone of their answers; black line = 95% range. Hover a bar for numbers, click it to see the theme on the map.2

Does the question set the tone?

Yes, more than who answered: answers on what limited participation were far less positive (odds ×0.11), answers on purpose and career more positive (×1.96). Region, school level, medals and awards add almost nothing.5

Where does each theme come from?

Each question fed mostly one theme, so a theme partly repeats what its question asked, and so does its tone.

Theme names, how stable each theme is, and what it can carry

Country

Does it change by country?

Two themes change with the country. “Financial Limitations and Funding Issues” most of all: 56% of people in Indian teams and 46% in Canadian teams raised it, and nobody in teams from Taiwan or Hong Kong.

The other is “Inspirational Yet Challenging Experience”; shorter answers explain part of it (see Region).6

Countries with fewer than 5 teams are pooled as “Other countries”. France, the host country with the most people per team, is shown in detail under Region.7

Region

Does it change by region?

One difference between regions shows. 73% of people in Asian teams raised “Inspirational Yet Challenging Experience”, against 54% in European teams. Shorter answers explain part of it, but not all.

Asian participants wrote shorter answers (median 11 words, against 13 in Europe), and short or one-word answers fall into this theme. With answer length and every other factor in one model, Asian teams still raise it more often (odds ×2.4; the model is under Trust).8

“Financial Limitations and Funding Issues” looks higher in North America (39%) than in Asia (14%), but this does not hold after correction. Inside Europe, nothing differs.

The project area does not change the picture either: across the nine villages with 8 or more teams, such as therapeutics or agriculture, no theme differs.

France, the host country, in detail

French teams said much the same as everyone else. Two differences: fewer French participants raised “Financial Limitations and Funding Issues” (11% against 22%) or “Inspirational Yet Challenging Experience” (51% against 64%).

French participants wrote longer answers (median 16 words, against 12 for the other teams). Short answers often fall into “Inspirational Yet Challenging Experience”, so this explains part of the second gap.

France has 57 participants in 7 teams, more people per team than any other country here. The comparison is France against all 118 other teams together.7

School level

Does it change with school level?

Two themes change with school level. “Financial Limitations and Funding Issues”: 27% of undergraduates against 3% of high-school students. “Strong Team Connections” goes the other way: 78% of high-school students against 57% of undergraduates.

The gap is 24 percentage points (95% range 15 to 33). Both results hold when any one team is left out. With region, medals and awards in the same model, the money gap points the same way but no longer passes correction (the model is under Trust).

School level and region overlap: 21 of the 23 high-school teams are Asian, and 30 of the 39 graduate teams are European. Only the model with all factors together can separate them, and with 125 teams it can do so only in part.

Awards and medals

Does any theme go with winning?

No theme goes with the award ladder. On the medal ladder there is one hint, for “Increased Confidence and Career Clarity”, but it does not hold in both regressions.

Regression A takes the ladder step as the outcome and allows for region, school level and team size. In it, teams where more people raised the theme had better medals: odds ×1.17 per 10 points (95% range 1.04 to 1.30, q = 0.04). Regression B looks only at the share per step up: +4.8 points per step (95% range −2.5 to +14.3, q = 0.95), which does not hold. The medal ladder is mostly gold (91 teams) and silver (25); only 9 teams had bronze or no medal. Read it as a hint to check in another year.9

Award ladder: no award 78 teams, nominated 33, special prize 14. Medal ladder: none 5, bronze 4, silver 25, gold 91; every step is kept, and the small steps are marked “few”.

Limits

How much can you trust this?

This is a first map of what was said, and it still needs a human check.

Repeating the grouping on random 80% samples agreed with the original at 0.68 for main themes and 0.70 for sub-themes (adjusted Rand index; 1 = identical). 30 of the 46 themes kept a Jaccard similarity of 0.75 or more over 25 refits. The flat map keeps near neighbours together at 0.89 (trustworthiness). 42 of the 46 names passed every check.

Machine reading, no human check yetA language model named the themes and read the tone. No person has coded a sample by hand to compare.
Topics follow the questions98% of the answers in “Financial Limitations and Funding Issues” answer the question on conditions. A theme partly echoes what a question asked.
Not every answer has a theme193 answers (13%) stay without one, mostly very short or mixed answers. Each answer gets one theme even when it touches two.
Who answered332 people from 125 teams who chose to reply, at one event. A difference between groups is a link; these data cannot show a cause.
For statisticians: every factor in one model

Answer length decides four of the seven themes. With every factor in one model, Asian teams still raise “Inspirational Yet Challenging Experience” more often than European teams (odds ×2.4); school level, medals and awards add nothing that passes correction.

Longer answers more often fall into “Strong Team Connections” (×3.3), “Increased Confidence and Career Clarity” (×2.3) and “Collaboration Challenges and Support” (×1.6). Short answers, often one word, fall into “Inspirational Yet Challenging Experience” (×0.48). The school-level money gap still points the same way (×13.8), but its range is very wide (1.8 to 107), because only 2 high-school people raised money.8

Notes and sources
  1. Each answer was described by 4,096 numbers with a multilingual model (Qwen3-Embedding-8B), in its own language and without translation. UMAP reduced these numbers for grouping and for the pictures; HDBSCAN found the sub-themes and Ward linkage merged them into main themes. A language model proposed the names, and a name was kept only if it passed separate checks. The same model wrote one sentence about each theme; the sentence is shown only when a check found its wording in the answers (31 of the 46 themes and sub-themes). ↩
  2. The density grouping (HDBSCAN) first left 403 answers unplaced. 200 of them were clearly close to one theme and were added to it, and 10 long answers that covered two topics were split. 193 answers stay without a theme. All figures use this final assignment (1,312 answers with a theme). A share of people is out of the 328 people with at least one themed answer (4 of the 332 have none). ↩
  3. Stability: the grouping was refitted 25 times and each theme’s return was measured with the Jaccard index; bands follow Hennig (2007). A theme is “describe only” when fewer than 15 participants voice it or when it is unstable. ↩
  4. We read every capitalised word and every part of every team name in the answers. Three people’s names, five cities that are also part of team names, a university, two labs, a research institute, another organisation, a sponsor and two project names were replaced. No team name, participant identifier or country is stored with an answer. Agreed with Marc Santolini, November 2026. ↩
  5. Tone was read by Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct in six levels, and the model had to quote the words its reading was based on. When the quoted words were not in the answer, the score was thrown away (30 times); 1,475 answers were scored. Here the six levels are shown as negative, neutral or mixed, and positive. ↩
  6. Group comparisons, on people, computed in notebook 4 (run of 9 November 2026): for each team, the number of people who raised each theme. For each theme, a chi-square statistic across groups is compared with 4,000 shuffles of the group label between whole teams, because people in one team answer alike; Benjamini–Hochberg correction over the 7 themes (or the 29 sub-themes voiced by at least 15 people). A ring marks a group whose adjusted standardised residual (Haberman, 1973) also holds after correction over all cells of the chart. Each result that holds was tested again with each team left out in turn. Intervals: 2,000 resamples of whole teams. Smallest detectable differences: 80% power, 5% level, with the design effect of the team grouping (one-way intra-class correlation). Groups have 5 or more teams, except bronze (4 teams). ↩
  7. France figure: notebook 4, the same base and tests as the rest of the page (France against all other teams, team-bootstrap ranges, correction over the themes). Notebook 3 also tested France on its own: Fisher tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction, an equivalence test with a margin of ±20 percentage points, and a PERMANOVA on the whole theme profile (R² = 0.003, p = 0.42). ↩
  8. Generalised estimating equations (Liang and Zeger, 1986), people grouped by team: one logistic model per theme with region, school level, the medal ladder, the award ladder, team size (log) and the person’s answer length (log of words), Benjamini–Hochberg correction over the themes for each factor. A factor whose group has nobody raising a theme is marked not estimable. Notebook 4. ↩
  9. Proportional-odds (ordinal logistic) model per theme at team level (McCullagh, 1980): award ladder none, nominated, special prize; medal ladder none, bronze, silver, gold. Predictor: the share of a team’s people who raised the theme (per 10 points), with region, school level and the number of respondents (log). Benjamini–Hochberg correction over the themes. Themes close to holding (q under 0.10) were checked with 500 shuffles of the ladder between teams; the ladder assumption was checked with separate models for each cut (the idea of Brant, 1990), and the medal ladder also as gold against the rest. Regression B: a linear regression of each person’s 0/1 (raised the theme) on the team’s ladder step, 2,000 resamples of whole teams for the range and 2,000 shuffles of the ladder between teams for the test, Benjamini–Hochberg over the themes. Notebook 4. ↩
