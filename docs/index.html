Reading thousands of answers and reports

Open survey questions and field reports produce thousands of short texts, and then someone has to read it all. Here, each dot is one text. Texts that say similar things sit close together, and each colour is a theme.

One dot per answer. Grey dots have no theme.

How it works

The same steps run on both collections.

  1. Clean the texts, but keep them as people wrote them.
  2. Describe each text with numbers that capture its meaning, using a multilingual model (Qwen3-Embedding-8B). Nothing is translated.
  3. Map the texts so that similar ones sit close together (UMAP), in three dimensions for reading and in more for grouping.
  4. Group the crowded parts of the map into sub-themes (HDBSCAN) and merge close sub-themes into main themes (Ward). Texts that fit nowhere stay unplaced, and we count them.
  5. Name each theme with a language model, and keep the name only if it passes separate checks.
  6. Compare groups of people or places, correcting for the number of tests.

What it cannot tell you

It does not replace reading. A count shows how often a topic comes up in the texts. How common it is in the world is another question. When two groups differ, we report a link and claim no cause.

Every page says which themes are unstable, which names failed their checks, and what was left out. No person has yet coded a sample by hand to compare with the machine; that is the next step.
